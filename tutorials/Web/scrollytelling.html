Introduction

Reshaping New York's Public Edge

Over the past two decades, New York City has fundamentally reimagined how it uses its public spaces. Waterfronts that were once industrial dead zones, elevated rail lines written off as eyesores, and traffic- choked plazas have been transformed into celebrated civic assets.

This map traces five sites that embody different models of that transformation. Scroll to explore each one.

Site 1 of 5 — Chelsea

The High Line

Opened in 2009 on a disused 1930s freight rail viaduct, the High Line runs 1.45 miles through Chelsea and Hudson Yards. It has become one of the most visited parks in the United States, drawing over eight million visitors annually.

The project is often cited as a textbook case of adaptive reuse and community-led advocacy. Friends of the High Line, founded by two local residents in 1999, spent a decade fighting demolition before securing city support and private funding.

Critics note that the park has also catalyzed dramatic neighborhood gentrification and rent increases in West Chelsea. It raises enduring questions about who public infrastructure is built for.

Site 2 of 5 — Midtown Manhattan

Times Square Broadway Plazas

In 2009, NYC DOT Commissioner Janette Sadik-Khan closed Broadway to through traffic between 42nd and 47th Streets as a six-month pilot, replacing moving lanes with folding chairs and temporary paint. The experiment was expected to worsen crosstown congestion. Instead, traffic improved citywide.

The plazas became permanent in 2010. They are one of the clearest examples of tactical urbanism scaling up to lasting infrastructure: low-cost, reversible interventions that demonstrate proof of concept before committing to permanent construction.

The redesign also shows the power of political will. The transformation required little engineering and virtually no new capital budget. The main ingredient was willingness to reallocate existing street space.

Site 3 of 5 — Williamsburg, Brooklyn

Domino Park

The five-acre Domino Park, designed by James Corner Field Operations (the same firm behind the High Line), opened in 2018 on the East River waterfront in Williamsburg. It preserved industrial artifacts from the former Domino Sugar refinery, including refinery equipment, as public art.

The park is the public-space component of a larger mixed-use development by Two Trees Management. It illustrates both the potential and the complications of privately funded public space: the park is owned and maintained by the developer, not the city.

What obligations do privately owned public spaces (POPS) carry? Who has the authority to regulate behavior, set hours, or determine programming? Domino Park sits at the center of these debates.

Site 4 of 5 — Long Island City, Queens

Hunters Point South Park

Hunters Point South Park was designed after Hurricane Sandy (2012) demonstrated the vulnerability of New York's waterfront to storm surge. The 11-acre park on the East River incorporates a "living shoreline": a planted berm that absorbs wave energy and provides habitat while protecting the neighborhood behind it.

The park is a case study in climate-adaptive infrastructure: the landscape itself is the flood protection system. This approach, sometimes called "green infrastructure" or "nature-based solutions," challenges the traditional distinction between parks (amenity) and infrastructure (utility).

At the same time, the park anchors one of the largest affordable housing developments in NYC history, illustrating how public space investment can be linked to housing policy goals.

Site 5 of 5 — Brooklyn

Prospect Park

Designed by Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux and opened in 1867, Prospect Park predates all the others by over a century. Olmsted himself considered it superior to Central Park, which he had completed just a few years earlier: its meadow is larger, its lake more naturalistic, and its borders more varied.

The park is a reminder that the design and political struggles around urban public space are not new. Olmsted spent years battling the Tammany Hall machine over funding, maintenance standards, and the park's mission. The arguments he made, that democratic cities require accessible green space for the physical and mental restoration of working people, are strikingly contemporary.

Prospect Park also raises questions of equity over time. Today it sits at the center of one of the most expensive residential neighborhoods in New York. In 1867, the surrounding land was largely undeveloped. Public parks don't just respond to cities; they shape them.

Conclusion

Patterns and Questions

Across these five sites, a few threads emerge. Public spaces are never just parks. They are instruments of urban policy, tools of economic development, and arenas of political contest. How they get built, who funds them, who maintains them, and who they serve are as important as their physical design.

For your own projects this semester, consider: what is the public space problem you're solving, and for whom? Who holds power in the process, and whose interests might be displaced?
